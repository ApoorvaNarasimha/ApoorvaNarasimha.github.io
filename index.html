about me

I'm an AI research engineer who likes turning experimental ideas into working code, scalable experiments and real-world systems. My core is research, programming and problem solving, and I only use AI to build things that are meaningful.

I love hard problems. My brain keeps circling them until they make sense. I strip them down first, build a fast feedback loop I can trust, and only then scale anything up.

At the University of Birmingham I run studies, review literature and build interactive, AI-enabled devices, which means collecting large datasets for them. At Risidio I built parts of the official websites with LLMs and image-generation models, and worked directly with clients. For my MSc dissertation I built soft robotics from scratch, which was the hardest part, and the project won an award from the university.

Great maths means nothing if it takes weeks to run. That's why I'm moving into AI systems research. I want to work on the memory and scheduling bottlenecks behind long-horizon AI agents, so models can reason over massive contexts and constant tool calls without leaving expensive GPUs idle.

I treat AI as a high-speed research assistant and a coding multiplier for work I can verify. Anything involving scientific truth, subtle correctness or high-stakes architecture stays under my control.

  1. Now · LIFT graphene sensors

    Wearable sensing with LIFT

    Printing flexible graphene sensors, testing how reliable they are, and using them for live hand interaction.

    • 5flexible substrates compared
    • 6properties tested
    • Livemovement sensing on skin
    How it was done: Wearable sensing with LIFT
    Why it matters
    Flexible sensors could put touch and movement sensing into soft things and onto skin, but they drift, age and behave differently on each material.
    Testing the sensors
    Printed graphene sensors with LIFT and compared them on five flexible substrates for resistance stability, deformation response, drift, recovery, ageing and repeatability.
    Using them live
    Built a real-time chord generator from flexible sensors on the skin of the hands, with a microcontroller and Python, checked against OpenCV and MediaPipe.
    What's next
    Collecting sensor data so the project can become AI-enabled, bringing LIFT printing into the xTool P3 laser workflow, and finishing the manuscript.
    With
    Dr Hyunyoung Kim, University of Birmingham.
    • LIFT printing
    • Real time
    • Microcontroller + Python
    • OpenCV · MediaPipe check

  3. 2024 · MSc dissertation

    MorphMind

    Can we predict how a soft interface will move, before it moves? The MorpheesPlug toolkit, rebuilt around motion capture and machine learning.

    • 500 Hzmotion capture
    • 5,000frames per recording
    • 3soft widget types
    How it was done: MorphMind
    Problem
    Soft materials morph in nonlinear, often inconsistent ways, and there was no easy way to track or predict those changes.
    What I built
    More repeatable soft widgets, a calibrated multi-angle motion-capture setup, and machine-learning models that predict each widget's deformation from inflation time and pressure.
    Result
    Recordings of 5,000 frames at 500 Hz. The fold changed length by about 1–2 mm and the spiral's closure by up to 140 mm, both regular enough to model. The teeth widget moved erratically, and hidden markers limited its predictions.
    • Motion capture
    • Machine learning
    • 3D-printed soft widgets
    • Spiral · fold · teeth

  4. 2023–24 · LLM evaluation · University of Birmingham

    Judging the judges

    How closely do a model's judgements of chatbot answers match a person's?

    • 80single answers scored
    • 40pairs compared
    • 2models against one annotator
    How it was done: Judging the judges
    What I did
    Compared single, pairwise and multiple-output designs for evaluating chatbot responses.
    How I measured it
    Agreement between human and model evaluations, using Pearson correlations in Python.
    Result
    Agreement was weak: mean r = 0.32 when scoring single answers (80 responses) and r = 0.21 when comparing pairs (40), between one annotator and two models.
    Why it matters
    An automated judge is only worth using if it agrees with the people it stands in for.
    • Python
    • LLM-as-a-judge
    • Human evaluation
    • Pearson correlation

  5. 2023–24 · Experiment design · University of Birmingham

    Where do eyes go when people cooperate?

    An eye-tracking study of the Stag Hunt game.

    • 31people took part
    • 30people analysed
    • < 0.08|r|, gaze and choice, 26 people
    How it was done: Where do eyes go when people cooperate?
    The study
    A Stag Hunt eye-tracking study with 31 students on a Tobii Pro Spark.
    What I did
    Briefing, consent, calibration and hypotheses, then fixation analysis in R. 31 people took part and one was excluded for a technical fault.
    Result
    For 26 of the 30 people analysed, time spent looking at each object was almost unrelated to how often their choice agreed with the other player's goal (|r| below 0.08).
    • Tobii Pro Spark
    • 31 participants
    • R
    • Fixation analysis
